## Distilled Summary

## Purpose
Amend Title 29 to require the State to preserve biological evidence for defined minimum periods, establish limits and procedures for lawful destruction of such evidence (including notice and waiting periods), create remedies when required evidence cannot be produced, and require statewide procedures and training on biological evidence preservation.

## Executive Summary (5-8 bullets)
- Requires the State to preserve **all biological evidence** in its possession from a case **as long as the crime remains unsolved OR as long as any convicted person remains in “custody,” whichever is longer**.  
- Extends preservation to cover cases where **any co-defendant convicted of the same crime remains in custody**; evidence may not be destroyed while any co-defendant remains in custody.  
- Allows destruction before those preservation periods expire only if **all conditions** are met, including: **>5 years after conviction is final and appeals are exhausted**, and the evidence is **not tied to a Class A–E felony**, plus **certified-delivery notice** and a **180-day waiting window** with no testing motion/request to retain.  
- Requires evidence to be retained in a manner that prevents **contamination, degradation, or reduction** of biological material.  
- Upon a defendant’s **written request**, the State must prepare an **inventory** of preserved biological evidence in that defendant’s case.  
- If required evidence cannot be located, the chief evidence custodian must submit an **affidavit under penalty of perjury** describing search efforts.  
- Courts must hold a hearing if required evidence is missing; if the court finds **intentional and willful destruction**, it must order a postconviction hearing and **infer DNA results would have been favorable** to the petitioner, plus impose other sanctions/remedies.  
- Requires the Delaware Police Accreditation Commission and Division of Forensic Science to **study, promulgate, and implement procedures within 120 days of enactment**; the Act is effective **30 days after enactment**.

## Pros (Potential Benefits)
- **Improves access to postconviction DNA review** by requiring preservation of biological evidence for as long as a convicted person remains in custody (and while crimes remain unsolved), increasing the likelihood evidence exists for later testing.  
- **Adds procedural safeguards before destruction**, including **certified notice** and a **180-day period** allowing motions for testing under **11 Del. C. § 4504** or written retention requests.  
- **Creates accountability mechanisms**: affidavit **under penalty of perjury** if evidence cannot be located, and judicial remedies/sanctions if destruction violates the statute.  
- **Standardizes practices statewide** by requiring procedures and training (collection, retention, cataloging, accessibility).

## Cons (Potential Drawbacks)
- **Increases storage and evidence-management burdens** because biological evidence must be preserved for long periods (potentially for the duration of incarceration/parole/probation/registration, or while a crime is unsolved), and also while **any co-defendant** remains in custody.  
- **Limits destruction options** by requiring multiple conditions (including the **>5-year** post-final-conviction threshold and **180-day** post-notice waiting period) before evidence may be destroyed for non-Class A–E felony matters.  
- **Operational burden of compliance**: creating inventories upon written request, providing certified-delivery notices to multiple recipients, and conducting/ documenting location efforts.

## Open Questions / Ambiguities
- The bill defines **“Custody”** to include **parole, probation, civil commitment, and sex offender registration**; it is not specified how long preservation must continue if a person remains subject to registration long after incarceration ends (i.e., practical duration implications are not further detailed).  
- The bill states the State is not required to preserve physical evidence when retention is **“impracticable”** due to size/bulk/character; it does not define **impracticable** beyond that description, leaving discretion to agencies.  
- “State” includes “private entities that perform such functions,” but the bill does not specify how obligations are enforced for contractors beyond the general definition and remedies framework.

## Key Policy Changes
- **Definitions expanded/clarified**:
  - “Biological evidence” broadly includes items that may contain identifiable biological material and applies whether the material is separately cataloged or present on other evidence (e.g., clothing, bedding, cups, cigarettes).  
  - “Custody” includes incarceration, civil commitment, parole/probation, and sex offender registration.  
  - “State” includes a wide range of public entities and private entities performing governmental/public evidence-related functions.
- **Mandatory preservation**:
  - Preserve all biological evidence in State possession from investigation/prosecution **for as long as the crime is unsolved OR any convicted person remains in custody, whichever is longer**.  
  - Preserve evidence while **any co-defendant convicted of the same crime remains in custody**.
- **Retention standards**: preserve to prevent contamination/degradation/reduction of biological material.  
- **Inventory requirement**: upon defendant’s written request, State must prepare an inventory of preserved biological evidence in the defendant’s case.  
- **Conditional destruction (all required)**:
  - **More than 5 years** after conviction is final and all appeals exhausted;  
  - Not related to **Class A–E felony** investigation/prosecution;  
  - No other law requires preservation;  
  - **Certified delivery** notice to (i) persons still in custody related to the evidence, (ii) their attorney of record or Office of Defense Services, (iii) Attorney General, and (iv) any other entity required by state law;  
  - No recipient, within **180 days** of receiving notice, files a testing motion under **11 Del. C. § 4504** or submits a written retention request.
- **Impracticable physical evidence**: if too large/bulky, State must remove and preserve portions likely to contain biological evidence in sufficient quantity for future DNA testing before returning/disposing of the rest.
- **Missing evidence remedies**:
  - Custodian affidavit under penalty of perjury describing efforts to locate required evidence.  
  - Court hearing required to determine whether destruction violated the statute and whether it was intentional/willful.  
  - If intentional/willful: court must order postconviction hearing, infer favorable DNA results for petitioner, and impose other sanctions/remedies.  
  - If negligent/non-willful: court may order postconviction hearing and/or impose sanctions/remedies.

## Who Benefits
- Defendants/petitioners seeking postconviction DNA testing or review, because biological evidence must be preserved longer and destruction is restricted.  
- Individuals in cases with co-defendants, because evidence must be preserved while **any co-defendant** remains in custody.  
- Courts and parties seeking reliable evidence integrity, due to required preservation methods preventing contamination/degradation.  

## Who May Be Negatively Affected
- State and local evidence-holding entities (including law enforcement agencies, prosecutors’ offices, courts, public hospitals, crime laboratories, and private contractors performing these functions) due to longer-term storage, notice, inventory, and compliance requirements.  
- Prosecutorial interests may face additional litigation risk/sanctions if evidence required to be preserved cannot be produced and destruction is found intentional and willful.

## Effective Dates and Deadlines
- **Effective date:** **30 days after enactment into law**.  
- **Procedure/training deadline:** Within **120 days of enactment**, the Delaware Police Accreditation Commission and Division of Forensic Science must study, promulgate, and implement procedures (in consultation with DOJ Criminal Division and Office of Defense Services).  
- **Destruction timing threshold:** Destruction (when otherwise permitted) cannot occur until **more than 5 years** after the conviction became final and all appeals are exhausted, and after required notice and a **180-day** post-receipt window passes without a testing motion or retention request.

## Enforcement, Penalties, or Compliance Requirements
- **Compliance requirements** include: preserving evidence for specified durations, maintaining evidence to prevent contamination/degradation, providing inventories upon written request, using certified-delivery notice before destruction, preserving portions of impracticably large/bulky evidence, and producing an affidavit under penalty of perjury if required evidence cannot be located.  
- **Court-enforced remedies for noncompliance**:
  - Mandatory hearing when required evidence cannot be produced.  
  - If destruction is found **intentional and willful**: mandatory postconviction hearing; court must **infer favorable DNA results** for the petitioner; and must impose other appropriate sanctions/remedies.  
  - If destruction is **negligent or non-willful**: court may order postconviction hearing and may impose sanctions/remedies.  
- **Penalty mechanism specified:** affidavit is **under penalty of perjury** (specific criminal penalties not detailed in the bill text).
