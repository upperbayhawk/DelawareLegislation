## Distilled Summary

## Purpose
Amends Delaware’s disorderly conduct statute (11 Del. C. § 1301) to add **intentional interference with the operation of a school bus** as a form of disorderly conduct, while creating specific **exceptions/clarifications** (including protections for authorized passengers and certain parents addressing health/safety concerns).

## Executive Summary (5-8 bullets)
- Adds a new disorderly conduct provision covering a person who **“intentionally interferes with the operation of a school bus”** while the bus is **transporting, picking up, or dropping off students**.  
- Defines covered interference to include: **refusing to exit** after a lawful order by the **bus driver or bus attendant**, **wrongfully restricting the bus’s movement**, or **threatening** the driver or a passenger (including while entering/leaving/waiting for the bus).  
- Clarifies that **a statement about hiring an attorney, seeking a legal remedy, or informing others about concerns is not a “threat”** for purposes of this new provision.  
- Provides that an **authorized passenger (including a student)** may **not** be guilty of disorderly conduct for violating the school-bus-interference paragraph.  
- Creates a specific carve-out for a **parent of a student with an IEP (IDEA, 20 U.S.C. § 1400 et seq.) or a Section 504 plan (29 U.S.C. § 794)** who is addressing a **health or safety concern** in a manner **safe for other occupants**, so they do not violate the “refuse to exit” or “restrict movement” subparts.  
- Leaves disorderly conduct classified as an **unclassified misdemeanor**; the bill expands the conduct included under that offense rather than creating a new standalone crime classification.  
- Notes the bill requires a **2/3 vote of members elected to each house** under **Delaware Constitution, Article IV, § 28**, because it expands the scope of an existing crime in inferior court jurisdiction.  
- Enacted as **Volume 85, Chapter 194** and **approved September 2, 2025**.

## Pros (Potential Benefits)
- **Improves school transportation safety and continuity** by explicitly criminalizing intentional actions that interfere with school bus operations during student transport/pickup/drop-off (new § 1301(a)(3)).  
- **Adds clearer enforcement hooks** for conduct on/around school buses (e.g., refusing to exit after a lawful order; restricting movement; threats), potentially aiding law enforcement and school districts in responding to disruptive incidents (new § 1301(a)(3)b.1–3).  
- **Reduces risk of over-criminalizing protected advocacy** by stating that indicating intent to **hire an attorney, seek legal remedies, or inform others** is **not a threat** (new § 1301(a)(3)b.3).  
- **Protects authorized riders** (including students) from being charged under this specific school-bus-interference provision (new § 1301(c)).  
- **Provides a disability-related safeguard** for parents of students with an **IEP or 504 plan** raising health/safety concerns, if done safely (new § 1301(d)(1)).

## Cons (Potential Drawbacks)
- **Expands criminal liability** by adding additional conduct to the disorderly conduct offense (new § 1301(a)(3)), potentially increasing arrests/charges for incidents involving school buses.  
- The “threat” prong may still be **fact-intensive** and could generate disputes over whether speech/actions constitute a threat versus non-threatening complaint activity, despite the explicit carve-out (new § 1301(a)(3)b.3).  
- The parent exception applies only to parents of students with **IEPs/504 plans** and only to **two** subparts (refusal to exit; restricting movement), not the “threat” subpart—potentially creating uneven treatment across families and scenarios (new § 1301(d)(1)).

## Open Questions / Ambiguities
- What conduct qualifies as **“wrongfully restrict[ing] the movement”** of a school bus is not further defined in the source text (new § 1301(a)(3)b.2).  
- The bill does not specify how to evaluate whether a parent is addressing a concern **“in a manner that is safe for the other occupants”** (new § 1301(d)(1)).  
- The penalty specifics for an **“unclassified misdemeanor”** are not stated in the source text (existing § 1301(b) retained).

## Key Policy Changes
- **New disorderly conduct basis:** intentional interference with school bus operation when the bus is transporting/picking up/dropping off students (§ 1301(a)(3)a).  
- **Enumerated acts of interference:**  
  1) boarding and refusing to exit after a lawful order by the **bus driver or bus attendant** (§ 1301(a)(3)b.1);  
  2) wrongfully restricting bus movement (§ 1301(a)(3)b.2);  
  3) threatening the driver or a passenger, including while entering/leaving/waiting (§ 1301(a)(3)b.3).  
- **Speech clarification:** statements about **hiring an attorney, seeking legal remedy, or informing others** are **not threats** for purposes of § 1301(a)(3)b.3.  
- **Authorized passenger protection:** an authorized passenger (including a student) cannot be guilty under § 1301(a)(3) (§ 1301(c)).  
- **Parent exception for disability-related plans:** parents of students with an **IEP** or **504 plan** addressing a health/safety concern safely do not violate § 1301(a)(3)b.1–2 (§ 1301(d)).  
- **Constitutional voting threshold noted:** requires **2/3 vote** in each chamber under **Article IV, § 28** to expand an existing crime (statement in act text).

## Who Benefits
- **Students and families** who rely on consistent and safe school bus transportation.  
- **School bus drivers and bus attendants** who receive clearer statutory protection against interference and threats.  
- **School districts and law enforcement** through clearer legal grounds to address disruptive bus-related incidents.  
- **Parents of students with IEPs/504 plans** raising health/safety concerns safely (limited exception in § 1301(d)).

## Who May Be Negatively Affected
- Individuals whose conduct near/on school buses falls within the new interference categories (refusal to exit, restricting movement, threats) and who may now face **disorderly conduct** charges (§ 1301(a)(3)).  
- Individuals engaged in contentious interactions around school buses where communications could be alleged as “threats,” though certain legal-advocacy statements are explicitly excluded (§ 1301(a)(3)b.3).
