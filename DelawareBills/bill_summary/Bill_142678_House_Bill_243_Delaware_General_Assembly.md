## Distilled Summary

## Purpose
Amend Title 14 (school taxes) to (1) authorize separate residential and non-residential school property tax rates within defined limits, (2) cap total revenue so districts do not collect more than otherwise authorized under law when using split rates, and (3) change the 10% add-on for delinquencies and collection costs from mandatory to optional (up to 10%).

## Executive Summary (5-8 bullets)
- Authorizes school districts to set **different residential and non-residential property tax rates** for school taxes. (§ 1916A(a))
- Requires the **non-residential rate** to be **at least equal to** the residential rate and **no more than 2 times** the residential rate. (§ 1916A(a))
- If a district uses split rates, it must apply the **same residential and non-residential rates across all taxes** authorized under **Chapter 19** or **Chapter 21** of Title 14. (§ 1916A(b))
- Establishes a **revenue-neutrality requirement**: total revenue under split rates **may not exceed** what the district would be authorized to collect if using a **single tax rate**. (§ 1916A(b))
- Changes current law so school boards **may** (not must) add **up to 10%** to the tax rate to cover **delinquencies and costs of collection**. (§§ 1913(a)(1), 1913(a)(1) second sentence; Synopsis)
- Clarifies boards may adjust tax rates based on updated annual assessments **so long as taxes collected do not exceed the total amount authorized** under **§ 1916A**. (§ 1913(a)(2); § 1913(b))
- Retains reassessment-related limitations that a recalculated rate may realize **no more than a 10% increase in actual revenue** over the relevant baseline. (§ 1916(b)(1)a.–b.)
- Makes technical drafting corrections to align with the Delaware Legislative Drafting Manual (as stated in the Synopsis).

## Pros (Potential Benefits)
- **Allows rate differentiation by property class** (residential vs. non-residential), enabling districts to structure tax burdens differently while staying within statutory bounds. (§ 1916A(a))
- **Explicit revenue cap** prevents split-rate adoption from increasing collections above what would be authorized under a single rate. (§ 1916A(b))
- **Flexibility on delinquencies/collection costs**: changing “shall add 10%” to “may add up to 10%” could reduce taxes compared with a mandatory add-on, depending on board choice. (§ 1913(a)(1); Synopsis)
- **Uniformity rule remains for certain cross-county districts** (districts traversing county lines) to keep real estate taxation uniform across the district, while still allowing adjustments constrained by the authorized total. (§ 1913(b))

## Cons (Potential Drawbacks)
- **Potential tax shift to non-residential property**: the non-residential rate must be at least the residential rate and can be up to **2×**, which could increase non-residential tax burdens relative to residential. (§ 1916A(a))
- **Reduced built-in cushion for delinquencies**: making the **up to 10%** add-on optional could reduce the amount collected for delinquencies and collection costs if boards choose a lower add-on. (§ 1913(a)(1); Synopsis)
- **Administrative complexity**: districts that choose split rates must apply them consistently across all authorized taxes under Chapters 19 and 21 and ensure total revenue does not exceed the single-rate authorization. (§ 1916A(b))

## Key Policy Changes
- **New authority to set separate rates**:
  - School boards may set a **non-residential tax rate different from the residential tax rate**. (§ 1916A(a))
  - Non-residential rate must be **≥ residential rate** and **≤ 2× residential rate**. (§ 1916A(a))
- **Uniform application across school taxes**:
  - If split rates are used, the district must use the **same residential and non-residential rates** for all taxes authorized under **Chapter 19 or Chapter 21**. (§ 1916A(b))
- **Revenue neutrality requirement**:
  - Total revenue from the two rates **may not exceed** what could be collected if using a **single rate**. (§ 1916A(b))
- **Delinquency/collection add-on becomes optional**:
  - Boards **may add up to 10%** for delinquencies and costs of collection (instead of being required to add 10%). (§ 1913(a)(1); Synopsis)
  - Conforming updates appear in § 1916(a) regarding inclusion of amounts authorized under § 1913(a). (§ 1916(a))
- **Assessment-based adjustment constraint**:
  - Based on updated annual assessments, boards may adjust rates **only if taxes collected will not exceed the total amount authorized to be collected under § 1916A**. (§ 1913(a)(2); § 1913(b))

## Who Benefits
- **School boards/school districts** that want the option to set **different rates for residential and non-residential property** within the statutory limits. (§ 1916A(a))
- **Taxpayers** in districts where boards choose to add **less than 10%** for delinquencies and collection costs (because the add-on becomes discretionary up to 10%). (§ 1913(a)(1); Synopsis)

## Who May Be Negatively Affected
- **Non-residential property owners/taxpayers** (business, commercial, industrial properties) in districts that adopt a higher non-residential rate (up to **2×** residential). (§ 1916A(a))
- **School districts** could face tighter cash-flow or collections management if they choose a lower delinquency/collection add-on than the previously mandatory **10%** (the bill authorizes but does not require the add-on). (§ 1913(a)(1); Synopsis)
