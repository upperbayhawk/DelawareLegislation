## Distilled Summary

## Purpose
Expand patient access to physical therapy services by extending how long a licensed physical therapist may treat a patient without a referral before a physician must be consulted, and revise physical therapist/athletic trainer license renewal, late renewal, and inactive/reactivation provisions to align with Division of Professional Regulation processes.

## Executive Summary (5-8 bullets)
- Extends the period a physical therapist may treat an individual **without a referral** before a physician must be consulted from **30 days to 90 days**.  
- Keeps the requirement that a physical therapist **refer the individual to another health practitioner** when symptoms are outside the physical therapist’s knowledge/scope.  
- Replaces prior statutory renewal mechanics (including **biennial expiration on January 1**, online renewal, automatic expiration, and a **5-year** expired-license renewal window) with a framework where renewal occurs **biennially** in a manner determined by the **Division of Professional Regulation**.  
- Requires renewal to include payment of the **appropriate fee** and **attestation** that continuing education requirements established by the Board have been met.  
- Authorizes the Board to set, by rule, a late-renewal period **not to exceed 1 year** after the renewal date.  
- Establishes that a licensee may be placed on **inactive status for no more than 5 years**; reactivation requires a request, a **fee set by the Division**, and proof of continuing education per Board rules.  
- Retains that knowingly or willfully making a false statement of fact in an application is subject to **prosecution for perjury** (not altered in the shown text).

## Pros (Potential Benefits)
- **Improved access and continuity of care:** Patients can receive physical therapy without a referral for up to **90 days** (up from **30 days**), reducing the need for early physician consultation solely to continue therapy.  
- **Administrative standardization:** Renewal and reactivation processes are revised to be handled “in such manner as is determined by the **Division**,” and described as aligning with other Title 24 boards and Division processes (per Synopsis).  
- **Clear late-renewal window:** Board must set a late-renewal period capped at **1 year**, which may provide a defined grace period for renewal.

## Cons (Potential Drawbacks)
- **Reduced statutory specificity on renewal timing and consequences:** The bill removes explicit statutory language specifying licenses “expire biennially on **January 1**,” “automatically expire” upon nonpayment, and the prior **5-year** expired-license renewal payment structure; the new statute relies more on Division-determined processes and Board rules.  
- **Potentially shorter pathway for late renewals than prior “expired” framework:** The bill directs the Board to set a late renewal period “not to exceed **1 year**,” while the prior text allowed renewal within **5 years** after expiration (though under a different “expired license” construct). ## Key Policy Changes
1. **Direct access duration extended:** Physical therapists may treat without a referral for up to **90 days** before a physician must be consulted (was **30 days**).  
2. **License renewal framework revised:**
   - Renewal is **biennial**, in a manner determined by the **Division**, with payment of the fee and **attestation** of meeting Board-established CE requirements.  
   - Board must set a late renewal period **not to exceed 1 year** after the renewal date.  
3. **Inactive status and reactivation standardized:**
   - Inactive status allowed for **no more than 5 years**.  
   - Reactivation requires a request, a **fee set by the Division**, and proof of CE per Board rules.  
4. **Removed/changed prior statutory mechanics (as shown by deletions):**
   - Deletes explicit reference to licenses expiring on **January 1**, automatic expiration upon nonpayment, online renewal language, and the former “renew within **5 years** after expiration” payment-per-year approach.
